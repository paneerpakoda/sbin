# Tip–surface contact: desk dents and spinner balance

Research checked 11 September 2026. Follow-up to Q12–Q17; no physical specimens or gameplay were tested. This note uses four primary sources and separates the user's experience from physical evidence and proposed game behaviour.

## The memory we are preserving

The user identifies the component as the **metal tip of a ballpoint refill**, not a fountain-pen nib. They remember centring, angle, tightness and insertion depth all affecting the assembly, without knowing which mattered most. A poor hole could waste otherwise good stationery. They also remember some sharp tips settling into desk dents and spinning for a long time, and different tips favouring different surfaces. These are firsthand observations of their school experience; they do not establish universal rankings or controlled comparisons.

## What the evidence supports

**Tip shape changes motion, but “sharper is better” is unsupported.** Rod Cross filmed the same aluminium disc with pointed and rounded supports and observed different behaviour. His account identifies contact friction as contributing to how a top rises towards upright. Consequently, friction is not simply a penalty to subtract from spin time: its direction, contact geometry and the motion at the contact matter. The specimens were purpose-made tops, not refill tips in rubber. [Rod Cross, University of Sydney](https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~cross/SPINNING%20TOPS.htm)

**Travel and spin should be measured separately.** Barthmann and Fischer experimentally studied three tops with approximately spherical bases on inclined supports. Their trajectories depended on the materials and inclination; a simple velocity-independent sliding-friction law did not explain the observed motion, so they proposed a slip-dependent model. This supports investigating each tip–surface pairing rather than assigning one universal “grip” score. It does not validate a particular desk-dent effect or supply values for school stationery. [Authors' paper, experimental setup and model](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11742)

**A recess can support a rotating pivot, but a desk dent is not a precision bearing.** Swiss Jewel documents cup bearings used with hemispherical pivots and specifies cup radius and depth. This is a real example of a concave support compatible with rotation. The narrower inference for Sbin is that confinement of sideways travel and resistance to rotation are different questions. A random dent may confine a suitably shaped tip, or introduce extra contacts and rubbing. None of these sources proves that a dent necessarily increases spin duration. [Swiss Jewel, cup bearings](https://www.swissjewel.com/products/jewel-bearings/cup-jewels/)

**A rigid-body approximation leaves out some construction failures.** RPI's graphics teaching material explicitly separates non-deforming rigid bodies from deformation models. Applying that distinction here: treating the eraser and refill as one fixed object cannot by itself predict rubber yielding, a hole enlarging, or the tip moving relative to the body. Effective loss and rebound parameters could approximate observed outcomes, but would require calibration and would not explain the material process. No eraser stiffness, friction or restitution values are established here. [RPI, rigid bodies and deformation, PDF pages 17 and 24–25](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/~cutler/classes/advancedgraphics/S23/lectures/08_rigid_body_deformation.pdf)

## Minimal controlled reference tests — proposed

Use already assembled specimens. Record overhead and side footage, dimensions, tip protrusion, visible fit and surface condition. Keep levelness and launch location consistent. Randomise condition order and retain failed launches. Start with five launches per condition as a small screening exercise, not a statistical guarantee.

| Pass | Conditions | Main comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface screen | Two distinct tip profiles × two locations on the same desk material: smooth and one existing shallow dent | Four conditions: compare duration, travel and wobble; record whether the tip enters, stays in or escapes the dent. |
| Fit screen | One reference pairing; baseline plus separate changes in centring, angle, tightness and depth | Five conditions: identify visible failure modes. Measure accompanying changes rather than assuming each adjustment is perfectly isolated. |
| Battle check | Best stationary pairing versus a mobile pairing; repeat with starting sides swapped | Can intentional incoming collisions dislodge or topple the stationary spinner? Does either pairing dominate? |

Record initial spin rate and tilt where footage permits; compare similar launches rather than pretending hand launches apply equal energy. Track time to first body contact, sustained wobble and complete stop separately. Also track path length and edge losses. Repeat promising findings across specimens before treating them as equipment rules.

## Main game risk — hypothesis

A dent that both preserves spin and prevents displacement could make the best strategy “park in the dent and wait.” That would weaken battles and launch variety. Test whether choosing the dent has an observable cost or counterplay before making it a strong feature. Candidate tradeoffs include difficult placement, limited attack reach and vulnerability to a particular incoming collision. These are game hypotheses, not established properties of refill tips. Avoid solving the problem with unexplained random ejections after launch.
